Nine Winters Ago

Nine years earlier, on the ninth of Frostmonth, Tomas Hale was fifty-two and his daughter was coming home.

Livia Hale was twelve that winter. She had spent the autumn with her aunt up the coast, learning to sew and hating it, and her letters had grown shorter and crosser until Tomas paid for her passage home on the Gannet, a fat-bellied coaster that made the run down the coast every fortnight. She was due in on the evening tide. Tomas had swept the cottage twice and bought a seed cake he could not afford.

The weather turned at noon. By four the wind had swung into the north-east and was driving the sea white across the black rocks that guarded the mouth of Corvel Quay. It was the kind of night the lamp was built for. Tomas lit it himself at half past four, trimmed the wick, wrote the hour in the Salt Ledger, and went down to the quay to watch for a red-and-white sail.

He watched for two hours. At a little before seven a boy came running along the harbour wall shouting that the lamp had gone out.

Tomas turned and saw it was true. The tower stood black against a blacker sky. He ran. He was fifty-two and he took the tower stairs three at a time, and when he reached the top the door of the lamp room was shut and locked. He hammered on it and shouted. No one answered. He knelt and put his eye to the keyhole, and on the other side he saw a small lantern moving, low down, as if someone were crouching on the floor with it.

"Who is there?" he called. "Open in the Guild's name!"

The lantern went out.

He had no key with him; he had left it hanging in the cottage, because he had never in his life needed it to get into his own lamp room. By the time he had run home for it and back again the door was standing open and the room was empty. The wick of the great lamp had been pinched out and the reservoir was full. Whoever had done it had not been in a hurry.

He relit the lamp. It was too late. From the gallery he could see lights on the water out beyond the rocks, where no lights should be, and then he could not see them anymore.

The Gannet went onto the rocks at a quarter past seven. Of the thirty-four souls aboard, eleven came out of the sea alive. One of them was a young mate named Odile Marr, who swam a line ashore through water that should have killed her and was afterwards given a medal by the town. Twenty-two bodies were found along the beach in the next three days.

Livia Hale was not among the living or the dead.

The sisters of Brannoc Abbey came down from their cliff with blankets and carts and took the survivors up to the infirmary, because there was nowhere else in Corvel Quay with enough beds. Tomas went up with them and walked the wards every night for a week, looking into every face. Sister Agathe, who kept the infirmary, held his hands and told him that the sea did not always give back what it took.

A week after the wreck they found a small green shawl caught on the rocks below the abbey. Tomas knew it at once; he had bought it in the market the spring before. The Guild entered Livia Hale's name in the register of the drowned, and the master of the Guild came to the cottage in person to give Tomas her condolences.

"These things are God's," she said. "The lamp is only a lamp, Tomas. Do not torment yourself."

He did not tell her about the lantern behind the locked door. He did not know why not, then. Afterwards he thought it was because of how she had said it, as if she had already decided what he was going to think.

He kept the lamp for nine more years. He trained an apprentice. He wrote the hour of lighting in the Salt Ledger every night, in his square careful hand, and every year on the ninth of Frostmonth he climbed the tower at dusk and sat beside the great lamp until morning, and let no one else touch it.
