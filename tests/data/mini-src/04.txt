Two Names

They buried Tomas Hale in the Guild plot behind the harbour chapel on a grey, windless morning. Master Sabine Oake read the Guild's words over the grave in her soft, even voice, and laid a sprig of rosemary on the coffin, and told Ines afterwards that the lamp would be given to a senior keeper until the Guild could decide what was best.

"Tomas left it to me," Ines said.

"Tomas was not the Guild," said Master Oake, and smiled with her mouth, and walked away.

Sister Wren had come down from the abbey for the burial. She stood apart from the Guild people, at the back of the crowd, in her plain grey habit. When it was over she came and took Ines by the arm and walked her down to the end of the harbour wall, where the wind took their words away from anyone else.

"I have to tell you something," she said, "and I have to tell it badly, because I have never said it aloud. Will you listen to all of it before you decide I am mad?"

"Yes," said Ines.

Sister Wren held up her left hand, with the white scar across the back of it.

"I got this on the rocks below the abbey," she said. "Nine years ago, on the ninth of Frostmonth. Sister Agathe found me there the morning after the wreck, half dead, with my hand cut to the bone. I did not know my own name. I did not know anything for nearly a year. The sisters kept me and taught me and called me Wren, because Agathe said I was small and brown and would not stop chattering once I started."

Ines said nothing. Her heart had begun to beat very hard.

"Things came back slowly," Wren said. "A seed cake. A green shawl. A man with square hands who wrote in a big book every night. When your Tomas walked into my archive three days ago I knew his face before he said a word, and I was so frightened I could not speak. My name was Livia Hale. Tomas was my father."

"Why didn't you tell him?" Ines whispered. "He looked for you for a week. He looked for you for nine years."

"Because of the other thing that came back," said Livia. "A week after the wreck, while I was still in the infirmary and did not know who I was, a tall grey woman came to the abbey and asked Sister Agathe whether any child had been brought in from the Gannet. Agathe said no. Afterwards she told me she had lied because she did not like the woman's hands. I saw the same woman yesterday, reading over my father's grave."

"Master Oake," said Ines.

"Master Oake," said Livia. "I meant to tell him. I meant to tell him that night in the cloister. And then he walked out to the gate, and I thought there would be time in the morning."

They stood together at the end of the wall while the tide turned under them. Ines took the other woman's scarred hand and held it, and found she could not think of anything to say that was large enough.

It was Bram who found them there. He came along the wall with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, and he did not smile.

"Ines," he said. "I need the key."

"What key?"

"Don't," said Bram. "Tomas gave it to you in the cloister. I know he did. The Guild wants it back, and Master Oake has promised me the keepership of the lamp if I bring it to her. Give it to me and nobody needs to get hurt."

Ines stared at him. "You were on the beach that night," she said slowly. "Below the stair. Before the bell rang."

"Give me the key, Ines."

She stepped back, and he caught her wrist. He was a head taller than her and had hauled Guild ropes since he was ten. Livia threw herself at his arm and he shoved her down onto the stones without looking at her. Then he reached into the neck of Ines's shirt, closed his fist round the iron ring, and pulled until the cord broke.

He stood for a moment with the brass key in his hand, breathing hard.

"I am sorry," Bram said. "I am. But you would never have given me anything, and she will."

He walked away along the harbour wall without hurrying. Ines knelt beside Livia and helped her sit up, and watched the boy she had learned her letters beside go up the hill towards the Guild hall, and understood that whatever he had been to her before, he was her enemy now.
