The Missing Page

The harbour lamp of Corvel Quay had burned every night for two hundred years, except one. Ines Varro knew the date of that night the way other girls knew their birthdays, because Tomas Hale had made her copy it into her first exercise book: the ninth of Frostmonth, the night the Gannet broke on the rocks.

Ines was nineteen and had been Tomas's apprentice for six years. Tomas was the keeper of the harbour lamp and a sworn member of the Lamplighters' Guild, and he was the only person in Corvel Quay who still climbed the tower stairs without stopping for breath. He was sixty-one. He said the stairs kept him honest.

That morning he did not climb them. Ines found him at the long table in the Guild hall with the Salt Ledger open in front of him. The Salt Ledger was the Guild's record of every night the lamp had burned: the hour of lighting, the oil used, the weather, the keeper's name. Nobody read it for pleasure.

"Look at this," Tomas said, and turned the book towards her.

She looked. The entries ran from the autumn of that year in Tomas's square hand, night after night, and then they stopped. Between the eighth of Frostmonth and the tenth there was only a narrow stub of paper close to the binding, cut so cleanly that it might have been done with a razor.

"The page for the night of the wreck," Ines said. "Someone has cut it out."

"Someone has cut it out," Tomas agreed. "And I would very much like to know who, and what they did not want us to read."

He closed the book gently, as though it were injured. Ines had never seen his hands shake before.

They were still sitting there when Bram Ostrow came in from the quay with salt in his hair. Bram was a journeyman of the Guild, two years older than Ines, and her oldest friend; they had learned their letters at the same bench and been beaten for the same broken window. He saw their faces and stopped.

"Who died?" he said.

"Nobody, yet," said Tomas. "Sit down, Bram. You may as well hear it."

Bram sat and listened. When Tomas showed him the cut page he whistled softly and ran his thumb along the stub.

"Nine years," Bram said. "Why would anyone bother now?"

"Because I have started asking questions," said Tomas. "I wrote to the Guild master last month. I asked her to open the inquiry again."

"Master Oake won't like that," said Bram.

"Master Oake did not like it," said Tomas. "She told me the matter was closed."

Sabine Oake had been master of the Lamplighters' Guild for eleven years. Ines had seen her only at the midwinter feast, a tall grey woman who smiled with her mouth and kept her hands folded. Ines did not think she had ever heard her raise her voice.

In the afternoon a ship came in under a red sail, and Ines went down to the quay to log her. The ship was the Kestrel out of the north, and her master came ashore herself, a broad woman in a blue coat with a silver ring in one ear. The dockhands called her the Captain and touched their caps to her.

"Odile Marr," the woman said, and shook Ines's hand like a man settling a debt. "Is old Tomas still keeping your lamp?"

"He is," said Ines. "Do you know him?"

"I was mate on the Gannet," said Odile Marr. "I was one of the eleven who came out of the water that night. Tell him the Captain of the Kestrel would like a word, when he has a moment. I have carried something in my head for nine years and I am tired of the weight."

Ines carried the message up the hill. Tomas listened without interrupting, and when she had finished he sat for a long while with his chin on his fists.

"There was a light," he said at last, very quietly. "That night. I have never told anyone. When I reached the top of the tower the lamp was out and the door to the lamp room was locked from the inside, and through the keyhole I saw a lantern moving. Someone was in there, Ines. Someone with a key."

"Who has keys to the lamp room?"

"Two people," said Tomas. "The keeper of the lamp, and the master of the Guild."

He did not say anything else, and Ines did not ask. But that night, for the first time in six years, she lay awake and listened to the sea, and wondered who had stood in the lamp room in the dark while the Gannet went onto the rocks.
