The Abbey Stair

Brannoc Abbey stood on the headland north of Corvel Quay, at the top of three hundred and twelve steps cut into the cliff. The sisters called it the abbey stair. Everyone else called it the widow-maker.

Tomas and Ines climbed it on the afternoon after the Kestrel came in. Tomas had spoken with Odile Marr for an hour that morning in the back room of the harbour inn, and had come out grey in the face and would not say what she had told him. All he would say was that the Guild sent a fair copy of the Salt Ledger up to the abbey every midsummer for safekeeping, and that the abbey's copies were never cut, because no one but the sisters ever saw them.

The archivist was a young woman of perhaps twenty-one, small and quick, with a white scar across the back of her left hand. The sisters called her Sister Wren. She took them down into a stone room under the chapel where the ledgers stood in rows on oak shelves, and she found the right year without looking at the labels.

"This one is sealed," she said, and showed them the wax. "Every book from that year is under the Abbess's seal. I cannot open it without her leave, and she is away at the chapter house in the north until the end of the month."

"Then I will wait until the end of the month," said Tomas.

Sister Wren looked at him for a long moment. Ines thought she was going to say something else. Instead she put the ledger back on its shelf and said, "Will you stay for supper? The fog is coming in, and the stair is no place to be in fog."

They stayed. The sisters ate in silence, and afterwards Tomas took Ines out into the cloister, where the fog was already so thick that the far arches had disappeared.

"Hold out your hand," he said.

She did. He put something into it, cold and heavy: an iron ring with a single long brass key, worn smooth at the bow. She knew it at once. It was the key to the lamp room.

"If anything happens to me, the lamp is yours," Tomas said. "Keep that on you. Do not give it to anyone. Not to the Guild, and not to Master Oake."

"Nothing is going to happen to you."

"Probably not," said Tomas. "But I am sixty-one and I have just started asking questions that someone drowned a shipful of people to stop. Humour me."

She put the key on its ring around her neck, under her shirt. Tomas nodded, as if a small job had been finished, and said he was going to walk to the gate and look at the fog.

He did not come back.

Ines waited an hour in the guest room, then went looking with a lantern. Sister Wren went with her without being asked. They found the abbey gate standing open and, at the top of the stair, Tomas's hat lying on the wet stone. They found Tomas himself at the bottom, three hundred steps down, where the stair turned on the last landing above the beach. He had been dead for some time.

Ines did not remember the next hours clearly. She remembered Sister Wren kneeling on the landing with her hands over her face. She remembered men with torches. And she remembered Bram Ostrow coming up from the beach path, soaked to the knees, saying he had heard the abbey bell and come to help.

"What were you doing on the beach in this?" she asked him.

"Looking for you," said Bram. "Both of you. You were not at the cottage." He put his arms round her, and she let him.

At first light the town watch came up from Corvel Quay. Their commander was Captain Fenn, a thin, careful man with ink on his cuffs who had been a clerk before he was a soldier. The Captain looked at the stair, and at the wet stone, and at the place where Tomas had lain, and wrote in a small black book.

"He slipped in the fog," the Captain said. "It happens on this stair every winter. I am sorry for your loss, Miss Varro."

"He climbed stairs every day of his life," said Ines. "He did not slip."

Captain Fenn closed his book. "Then tell me who pushed him," he said, not unkindly, "and I will go and arrest them."

She could not. She stood at the top of the widow-maker with the brass key cold against her breastbone and found that she could not say a single name.
