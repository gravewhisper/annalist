The Salt Ledger

Livia Hale broke the Abbess's seal that night by candlelight, in the stone room under the chapel, with Ines holding the candle.

"She will forgive me," Livia said. "Or she will not. Either way my father is dead and I am done being quiet."

The fair copy of the Salt Ledger for the year of the wreck was written in a clerk's round hand, not Tomas's, but every column was there: the hour of lighting, the oil, the weather, the keeper's name, and, at the far right, a narrow column headed Keys Drawn, where the Guild recorded every time a key to the lamp room left the hall. Ines had never looked at that column in her life. Nobody did.

Livia turned the pages to the ninth of Frostmonth and put her finger on the line.

Lit at half past four by T. Hale. Wind north-east, rising. And in the narrow column at the edge of the page: Master's key drawn at six o'clock, S. Oake.

They looked at each other across the candle.

"That is what she cut out," Ines said. "Not the lamp. The key."

They went down the widow-maker at dawn with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth, and they did not go to the Guild hall. They went to the watch house on the quay, and they found Odile Marr already there, sitting on a bench in her blue coat with her arms folded.

"I told Tomas what I saw," she said, when Ines asked. "I suppose I had better tell someone who is still alive. That night, when I came up out of the water with the line in my teeth, the first thing I saw was a grey Guild barge pulling away from the foot of the lamp tower with no light in her. I knew the barge. Everyone in Corvel Quay knew it. It was the master's."

Captain Fenn read the ledger line twice. Then he wrote in his small black book for some time, and closed it, and put on his sword.

"Come with me," the Captain said. "All three of you. Captain Marr, you will tell it again in front of her."

They found Sabine Oake in the Guild hall, at the long table, with Bram Ostrow standing at her shoulder. The cut ledger lay open between them. Bram saw Livia come through the door behind the watch and went white, as if he had seen a drowned girl walk out of the sea, which in a sense he had.

Fenn laid the abbey's copy on the table beside the Guild's own, open at the ninth of Frostmonth, and put his finger on the narrow column.

Master Oake looked at it for a long time. Then she folded her hands.

"There was an auditor aboard the Gannet," she said, in her soft, even voice. "He was coming down from the north to examine the Guild's accounts. I had been taking from them for two years, and he would have found it in a week. I put out the lamp. I did not think about the others. I am not sure I think about them now."

Nobody spoke. Out on the quay a gull was screaming.

It was Bram who broke. He sat down very suddenly on the bench by the wall and put his face in his hands.

"She told me to get the key from Tomas," he said. "That was all. Get the key, and the lamp would be mine. I followed him out of the abbey gate in the fog and asked him for it, and he said he did not have it, and I did not believe him. I took hold of his coat. He pulled away from me at the top of the stair and the stone was wet." He looked up at Ines. "I did not push him. I swear I did not push him. But I was holding his coat when he went."

Captain Fenn arrested Sabine Oake for the wreck of the Gannet and the deaths of twenty-two people, and Bram Ostrow for the death of Tomas Hale. The brass key to the lamp room was still in Bram's pocket when the watch led him away.

That evening Ines Varro climbed the tower stairs of Corvel Quay without stopping for breath, as Tomas had taught her. Livia Hale climbed beside her. At half past four Ines lit the harbour lamp and wrote the hour in the Salt Ledger, in a hand that was not yet as square as her master's, and below it, in the column for the keeper's name, she wrote both of theirs.
