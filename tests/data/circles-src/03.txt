The Visitor from Gloam

In the first week of winter a woman in a lamp-black cloak climbed the spit and asked for Corrow by name. She was Hennet Larrow, and she had come from the Gloam School in the hills beyond the marsh.

Corrow came up from the cellar with his boots dripping and his face set hard, and then he saw who it was and stopped on the top step as if he had forgotten how stairs worked. The two of them looked at each other for a long moment. Then Hennet said that his beard had gone grey, and Corrow said that her cloak had gone shabby, and they went into the small parlour together and shut the door.

Iselde brought them tea and stayed by the door to listen.

"You still call yourselves Gloam," Corrow said. "After all these years."

"Gloam is the name of our school and the name of its first circle," Hennet said. "Our founders named the house after the first door. Every student of Gloam begins as a Gloam, and most of them never mind the confusion."

Corrow laughed, which Iselde had never heard him do. "And the Wickbearer elixir? Do you still brew it with candle fat?"

"We brew it with beeswax now," Hennet said. "We have become very modern."

They talked late into the night about people Iselde had never met: a teacher who had drowned, another who had married a lighthouse keeper and gone north, a boy who had run off with the fishing fleet and come back ten years later with a parrot and a debt. Iselde refilled the teapot three times. Near midnight Hennet turned to her and asked what she had learned so far, and Iselde told her about the tide in her wrists. Hennet listened gravely, as if it were the most interesting thing she had heard all year, and then said that the marsh between their schools was full of tides too, only slower, and that one day Iselde might come and feel them for herself.
